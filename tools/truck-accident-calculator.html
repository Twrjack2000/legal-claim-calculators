How 18-Wheeler & Commercial Truck Settlements Are Valued

Commercial trucking collisions are governed by federal regulations under Title 49 of the Code of Federal Regulations (49 CFR Parts 382–399) enforced by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA). Unlike passenger car crashes—where recovery is frequently capped by a $25,000 or $50,000 state minimum auto policy—interstate motor carriers must carry a minimum of $750,000 for general freight, $1,000,000 for oil/hazardous materials, and up to $5,000,000 for bulk hazmat, often backed by multi-million-dollar excess and umbrella towers.

When an attorney proves that a motor carrier violated federal safety mandates—such as forcing a driver to exceed the 11-hour driving limit or 14-hour on-duty window, ignoring out-of-service brake defects, or hiring a driver with disqualifying safety violations—the case shifts from ordinary negligence to negligent entrustment and gross negligence. This unlocks punitive damage exposure and drives settlement multipliers from standard 2×–3× levels up to 4×–6× total economic specials.

Frequently Asked Questions

Because commercial semi-trucks weigh up to 80,000 lbs and are required by federal law (FMCSA) to carry at least $750,000 to $5,000,000 in liability insurance, settlements are significantly higher than standard car crashes. Moderate injury cases involving surgery often settle between $150,000 and $650,000, while severe injuries or cases involving hours-of-service (ELD) or maintenance violations frequently exceed $1,000,000 to $5,000,000+.
The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) enforces strict rules on driver Hours of Service (49 CFR Part 395), CDL qualification, drug/alcohol testing, brake inspections, and cargo securement. Proven FMCSA violations establish negligence per se or gross negligence, exposing the motor carrier to punitive damages and forcing commercial insurers to pay near or at policy limits to avoid runaway jury verdicts.
Unlike standard auto accidents, commercial truck crashes often involve multiple deep-pocket defendants: the truck driver, the motor carrier (trucking company), the freight broker or shipper, the trailer owner, third-party maintenance contractors, and cargo loaders. Identifying multiple liable entities allows attorneys to stack commercial insurance policies.
Modern commercial trucks contain an Electronic Control Module (ECM) and Electronic Logging Device (ELD) that record speed, braking, throttle, GPS, and driver hours right before impact. Trucking companies can legally overwrite or destroy this data in as little as 30 days unless an attorney serves a formal Spoliation / Preservation Letter immediately after the crash.
